Understanding LSTM autoencoders for anomaly detection

Lathika Sakthivel · 20 November 2025 · from the AutoEDA project

In AutoEDA I used LSTM autoencoders to find anomalies in multivariate time-series. The point was not to decorate a notebook — it was to flag unusual patterns without needing a pile of labeled incidents.

What autoencoders actually do

An autoencoder is an unsupervised network with two jobs:

  • Encoder — compress the input into a smaller representation.
  • Decoder — rebuild the original input from that representation.

Training minimizes the gap between input and reconstruction. Normal points reconstruct cleanly. Odd points do not. That gap is the signal.

Why LSTM for sequences

Plain autoencoders are fine for static rows. Time-series is a sequence. LSTMs are built to keep long-range dependencies, so an LSTM autoencoder can reconstruct a window of time instead of a single snapshot.

How it sat inside AutoEDA

The TensorFlow model took multivariate windows and ran them through:

  1. An input layer for the multivariate sequence
  2. LSTM encoder layers that collapse the window into a vector
  3. A bottleneck for the compressed state
  4. LSTM decoder layers that rebuild the sequence
  5. An output layer for the reconstruction

It learned on ordinary data. At inference, reconstruction error above a threshold meant “look at this.”

What that bought

  • No labeled anomalies required for training
  • Several series at once, not one column at a time
  • Temporal structure actually used, not flattened away
  • An error value you can show a person, not just a binary flag

The same idea shows up in fraud, maintenance, intrusion detection, and factory quality checks — anywhere “normal” has a shape and “wrong” is rare.

Closing

LSTM autoencoders are useful when the dataset is sequential, labels are scarce, and you need a reason to look closer. In AutoEDA they were one piece of a larger automation loop, not the whole product.
